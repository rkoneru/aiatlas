Human-in-the-loop controls for agents

Autonomy is earned, not granted. Eight ways to keep people in control as agents take on more work, from approving each action to a switch that stops everything. Match the control to the risk, not to the technology. Walk one agent action through the controls.

Eight controls

Before acting

1 · Approval gate

The agent proposes; a person approves before anything runs. A rejection goes back to the agent with the reason. Every decision is logged.

Best for: payments, contracts, messages to customers.
Watch: approval fatigue, where review turns into rubber-stamping.

Before acting

2 · Confidence routing

Only low-confidence outputs go to a person; the rest go straight through. Outcomes are logged and used to recalibrate the threshold.

Best for: high-volume triage of claims, tickets and invoices.
Watch: confidence scores that were never checked against real outcomes. A model's own confidence is often poorly calibrated.

After acting

3 · Sampled review

Everything runs automatically. Reviewers check a random slice, say 5%, every week, score it, and feed the failures into the eval set and prompts.

Best for: mature, low-risk flows that are already stable.
Watch: samples too small to catch rare, costly errors. Sample more from high-value cases.

When stuck

4 · Exception queue

Rules catch what the agent can't handle, such as missing data or a policy flag, and park it for a person. Everything else goes straight through.

Best for: rules-heavy operations: identity checks (KYC), invoices, onboarding.
Watch: queue backlogs that quietly breach the service level.

When stuck

5 · Escalation path

Clear triggers (high value, an angry customer, an unknown intent) hand the case up a ladder: operations, then a specialist, then the risk owner. Each step gets the full context.

Best for: customer service, complaints, claims disputes.
Watch: handoffs that make the customer repeat everything.

Before acting

6 · Dual control

Two different people must sign off before a high-risk action runs: one prepares or reviews (maker), the other approves (checker). If either declines, the action is rejected.

Best for: payments above a limit, regulated decisions.
Watch: it's slow. Keep it for the top risk tier only.

After acting

7 · Audit replay

Every agent step (prompt, data, tool calls, output) goes into a tamper-proof, timestamped trace. A reviewer can replay any decision; a flawed one leads to a fix in the policy or prompt.

Best for: regulated decisions and reviews after an incident.
Watch: gaps in what's logged, especially tool calls and retrieved data.

Always on

8 · Kill switch

A monitor watches cost, error rate and drift. When a limit is breached, a circuit breaker pauses the agent and hands the work back to people; it resumes only after review.

Best for: every production agent, without exception.
Watch: a switch nobody has tested. Rehearse it.

Making oversight real

Give reviewers what they need

Show the proposed action, the evidence behind it, what the agent was unsure about and what happens if it's wrong. A bare "Approve?" button invites rubber-stamping.

Measure the reviewers too

Track approval rate, time per review and how often reviewers overturn the agent. Approval near 100% with seconds per review means the gate has stopped working.

Earn autonomy with evidence

Move a task down a tier only after a set number of clean runs, for example from approval gate to sampled review after 500 approvals with no corrections. Move it back up after an incident.

Human review alone won't scale

At thousands of actions a day, use automated checks and evaluator agents for most of the work, and keep people for the decisions that matter. See Agents watching agents and its review-load calculator.

Regulation points the same way. For high-risk systems, the EU AI Act (Article 14) requires effective human oversight, including the ability to override the system's output and to stop it. See Responsible AI, regulation & compliance and AI guardrails.
