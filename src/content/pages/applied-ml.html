Applied · Classic ML

Classic ML applications: recommenders, forecasting & vision

Much of the AI that runs businesses isn't generative at all. Recommenders decide what you see, forecasts decide what gets stocked and staffed, and computer vision inspects, counts and reads. LLMs increasingly sit beside these systems rather than replacing them. Step through a two-stage recommender.

Recommendation systems

Collaborative filtering

"People who liked X also liked Y." Matrix factorization learns a vector per user and per item; their dot product predicts affinity.

Content-based

Recommend items similar to what the user engaged with, using item features or embeddings. Handles new items (cold start) better.

Two-stage retrieval + ranking

Two-tower models retrieve a few hundred candidates from millions, then a heavier ranker scores them with rich features. Business rules re-rank for diversity and freshness.

Forecasting & time series

Statistical baselines

Seasonal naive, exponential smoothing (ETS) and ARIMA. Always benchmark against them; they're hard to beat on short, clean series.

ML & deep models

Gradient boosting with lag and calendar features excels with many related series. Deep models (N-BEATS, temporal fusion transformers) and pre-trained time-series foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos) forecast zero-shot.

Backtesting

Evaluate with rolling-origin splits: train up to week t, forecast t+1…t+h, slide forward. Report MAPE or WAPE and prediction intervals, not just a point forecast.

Where LLMs fit: explaining a recommendation, turning a forecast into a narrative and action plan, or letting users query a vision system in plain language. The core prediction usually stays with the specialized model, which is cheaper, faster and easier to evaluate.

Robotics & embodied AI

Robots are where perception, language and action meet the physical world, and where mistakes can't be undone with a retry.

Vision-language-action models

From instruction to motor command

VLA models take camera images and a natural-language instruction ("put the red cup in the sink") and output robot actions directly, building on vision-language model backbones. Generalist robot policies are trained on large pooled datasets from many robot types.

Sim-to-real & world models

Practice in simulation

Physical data is slow and expensive, so robots train in physics simulators with randomized lighting, textures and dynamics, then transfer to the real world. World models learn to predict how a scene will change, which lets a robot plan by imagining outcomes.

Hierarchy

Slow thinking, fast control

A common design: an LLM or VLM plans high-level steps at a few hertz, while low-level controllers run hundreds of times per second for balance and grasping. The same split appears in self-driving stacks.

Safety

Bounded autonomy

Hard safety limits in classical control (force, speed, geofences) sit below any learned policy, with emergency stops and human supervision. Functional-safety standards and, in the EU, product and AI rules apply. See AI compliance.
