AI infrastructure, compute & sovereignty

Every AI strategy rests on compute: who provides it, where it runs, what it costs and what happens when it's scarce. This page helps leaders choose between APIs, cloud platforms and owned infrastructure, plan for capacity and power, and decide how much sovereignty they need. Walk the options from API to owned hardware.

What drives the cost

Tokens, not servers

For API use, cost is volume × tokens × price. The price of a given capability level has fallen steeply year after year, while the newest frontier models stay premium. Plan for both. See AI budget & TCO.

Utilization

Owned or reserved GPUs only beat APIs when they're busy most of the time. A cluster running at 20% utilization costs five times as much per token as the same cluster fully used.

Memory, not just chips

Large models and long contexts are limited by accelerator memory and bandwidth. Quantization, batching and caching change capacity needs by multiples. See LLM optimization & serving.

Agents multiply usage

An agent can make dozens of model calls per task, and reasoning models spend extra tokens thinking. Forecast from tasks per month × calls per task, not from chat messages. See Reasoning models.

Capacity, power & supply

Demand for AI compute has outrun supply. Accelerators have long lead times, new data centers are limited by grid connections and power, and providers ration capacity through rate limits and commitments. For most companies this shows up as rate limits, gaps in regional availability and price changes rather than as a hardware shortage.

Plan for limits

Negotiate rate limits and throughput in contracts, use more than one region or provider for critical workloads, and design graceful fallback to a smaller model.

Energy & emissions

AI workloads raise energy use and may fall inside your emissions reporting. Ask providers for energy and carbon data per region, use efficient models for high-volume tasks, and don't run a frontier model where a small one will do.

Sovereign AI

Sovereignty isn't one requirement. Separate the questions:

DimensionQuestionTypical answers
Data residencyWhere are prompts, outputs and logs stored and processed?Regional endpoints, no training on your data, retention limits
OperationalWho can access the systems, and from where?Local operations staff, customer-managed keys, access logs
LegalWhich country's law can compel access?Local legal entity, contract terms, choice of provider
TechnologicalCould you keep running if a provider cut you off?Open-weight fallback models, portable architecture, more than one provider
StrategicDoes the country or sector need its own capability?National or sector models, local compute, local-language data
Most organizations need data residency and contractual controls, not their own data center. Decide per use case: assistants over public data can use global APIs; regulated or classified workloads may need regional, dedicated or on-premises options.

Questions for the board

  • Which AI workloads would stop if our main model provider had an outage or changed its terms?
  • What share of AI spend is committed versus pay-as-you-go, and how well used is committed capacity?
  • Which workloads have residency or sovereignty requirements, and how are they met today?
  • How are AI energy use and emissions measured and reported?
